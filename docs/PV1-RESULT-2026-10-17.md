## What this means

**Manu 12.113, read as a learning rule, fails.**

- **The rule tested:** a single member that *knows* (its answer rests on established lessons) overrides the agreeing others.
- **It lost:** −0.67 pp. Over 2,920 overrides, the member was right 965 times and the agreeing pair 1,657 times.
- **It also did not beat following the member at random** (+0.07). So the member's own sense of knowing is not a reason to overrule a diverse assembly.
- **Where it helped and where it hurt:**
  - It helped on noisy defect and process data, where lessons are sound: pc3, cylinder-bands, kc2.
  - It failed badly on semeion handwritten digits (−6.9 pp). There, lessons that held on six or more cases were overruled correctly by naive Bayes and the tree 745 times out of 893.
- **What holds instead:** the plurality of members of different kinds (12.110–112). That assembly beat its own lesson member again, by +1.17 pp, on a fourth fresh pool.

**Silencing the guesser (12.114) costs.** Where the lesson member only guessed and withdrew from the vote, the decision got worse: −3.20 pp, and −14.9 on vowel. The nearest-case guess is a better tie-breaker than naive Bayes alone.

**What works: an assembly that asks only under doubt.**

- **The rule:** all members observe a label only when they disagree or the lesson member doubts.
- **Against random asking at the same number of labels:** +3.94 pp, on all nine datasets.
- **Against the assembly that saw every label:** it matched it (+0.01) with 55% of the labels.

This is JK-1's jijñāsā lifted to the whole assembly, in the form of query by committee.

**Taken together with JK-1:**
- Knowing where one knows is valuable for **asking** and for **abstaining** (JK-1: +3.69 and +7.08 pp).
- It is not a licence to **overrule** others who agree against you.

## What this means

**Dismissal is a one-culprit rule.**
- Arthaśāstra 1.12 dismisses "the spies concerned" when they frequently differ. The mapping counted, for each spy, how often it was the odd one in 2-against-1 splits.
- With two broken spies, most reports are three-way splits, and the blame for the remaining splits is shared by two culprits. Neither reaches 20 of 30, so no one is dismissed. Once, the working spy was out-voted by two broken ones agreeing by chance, and was dismissed instead.

**What goes wrong then.**
- Two broken spies trained on noise still lean toward the commonest label. Sometimes both agree with the working spy when it is wrong, and the network answers wrongly alone.
- The supervisor's other pieces (saṃskāra, resets) make it answer more, and so err more: 2.00 per 100, against the plain network's 0.84.
- It is still right 95.2% of the time when it answers alone, but that is past the preregistered safety bound.

**The cost of answering more, generally.** The supervisor also errs more than the plain network when nothing fails (0.59 vs 0.12 per 100) and when one source fails (1.25 vs 0.22). This is the price of its higher coverage. MX-5 showed the same, and net credence counts it.

**For a working system (a rule to add, not yet tested).** Watch the system's whole agreement rate, not only each source's disagreements. If the share of cases answered alone falls far below its own baseline (prakṛti–vikṛti, PK-1, on coverage), at least two sources may be broken. Then:
- tell the owner;
- stop answering alone until a source is repaired.

Here, coverage fell from 49.6% to 13.7% in TWO, a signal that should be easy to catch. That rule needs its own preregistered test.
